Look Up and Down

Point your phone up to see the planets, stars and constellations above you. Point it down and see straight through the Earth to the other side of the world, as if the planet were glass.

On an iPhone, hold the phone flat over the floor for a moment first, so the compass can settle.

Your GPS position and the camera picture stay on your phone. Only place names you search for are looked up online.

Where are you looking from?

Searches are looked up by Photon (OpenStreetMap data, run by komoot), or Open-Meteo's place finder if Photon fails: only the words you type are sent. Your GPS position never leaves your phone.

Find something

Pick one and the view turns to it, or, with the compass, an arrow shows which way to point. Countries are found straight through the Earth.

How it works

Point the back of your phone anywhere and this page shows what lies that way. Above the horizon it is the sky as it is right now from where you are: the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the brighter stars, the constellations and the Milky Way.

Below the horizon, imagine the Earth were made of glass. Look down at your feet and you would see straight through it to the far side of the world, from underneath. The page draws the countries and oceans in that direction, through the planet. Look straight down and you see the exact opposite side of the world; tip the phone towards the horizon and the view sweeps closer to home.

The sky

The sky is the same as on orrery.live's night sky, worked out on your phone for this moment: by day it is blue and the stars are hidden, as they really are. Things below the horizon, such as the Sun at night, keep their names, faint, where they sit beyond the far side of the Earth.

The buttons

Find searches the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the named stars, the constellations, the countries and the oceans; pick one and the view turns to it, or an arrow shows which way to point the phone. Earth hides the glass Earth to show the whole sky, including the half below the horizon, as a dark sky with its names. Cam shows the camera picture behind the view. Tap the place at the top left to look from somewhere else.

Why it looks back to front

You are seeing the far side from inside the Earth, so the map is the mirror image of the one you know, just as a sign reads backwards from behind a window. The names are written the right way round so you can read them.

The glow

The blue haze towards the edge of the view is the atmosphere, seen at a slant where your line of sight skims the far surface. Look steeply down and you will see faint orange rings: the Earth's liquid outer core and solid inner core, which your line of sight passes through on the way.

Your privacy

Your GPS position and the camera picture are used only on your phone and never leave it. Find searches only on your phone. When you search for a place to look from, the words you type are sent to Photon (OpenStreetMap data, run by komoot), or to Open-Meteo's place finder if Photon fails, and nothing else. A link that starts somewhere else carries that place after a #, which browsers keep to themselves rather than sending to the website.

How it's worked out

The satellite picture is NASA's Blue Marble (public domain), and places come from Natural Earth's country outlines, the same as on What's Across. Your phone's compass is corrected from magnetic to true north with the World Magnetic Model 2025. The Earth is treated as a perfect sphere, and phone compasses are only good to a few degrees, so a far-away place can be a few hundred km off. The layers the line of sight passes through come from the Preliminary Reference Earth Model (Dziewonski and Anderson, 1981).

The planets come from the VSOP87 planetary theory and the Moon from Meeus's lunar theory, both checked against JPL Horizons. Star data: HYG database (CC BY-SA 4.0). Constellation figures: d3-celestial (BSD-3). Constellation boundaries: IAU (Delporte 1930) via Roman (1987), CDS VI/42. Milky Way: NASA/Goddard Space Flight Center Scientific Visualization Studio, Deep Star Maps 2020; Gaia DR2: ESA/Gaia/DPAC.

Questions

What is on the other side of the world from me?

Point your phone straight down: the place in the middle of the view is the exact opposite side of the world from where you are, called the antipode. For most places it is sea. From Brisbane it is the Atlantic Ocean, about 880 km west of the Canary Islands; from London it is the Pacific, about 220 km from New Zealand's Antipodes Islands; from Madrid it is New Zealand itself.

What planets can I see tonight?

Point your phone up. The Sun, the Moon and the planets are shown and named where they are in your sky right now, with the brighter stars, the constellations and the Milky Way. By day the sky is blue and the stars are hidden, as they really are. For tonight's planets with rising and setting times, see orrery.live's night sky.

How far is it through the Earth?

Straight down to the opposite side is 12,742 km, the width of the Earth, through the crust, the mantle, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core. Wherever you point, the card gives the distance in a straight line, the distance round the surface, and the deepest layer the line passes through.

Why does the map look back to front?

You are looking at the far side of the Earth from inside it, so the map is the mirror image of the one you know, just as a sign reads backwards from behind a window. The names are written the right way round so you can read them.

Can I find a planet, a star or a country?

Yes. Tap Find and start typing: the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the named stars, the constellations, the countries and the oceans are all there. Pick one and the view turns to it, or, with the compass, an arrow shows which way to point your phone. Countries are found straight through the Earth.

Does it work on iPhone, Android and computers?

Yes, in Safari, Chrome, Firefox or Edge. On a phone, tap Start and allow motion and location when asked; on an iPhone, hold the phone flat over the floor for a moment so the compass can settle. On a computer, or without a compass, drag to look around and scroll or pinch to zoom.

Does it work without signal?

Yes, apart from the place search. Open it once with a connection, and after that the sky and the glass Earth work without one. To keep it handy, add it to your home screen: in Safari tap Share, then Add to Home Screen; in Chrome, choose Install app or Add to Home screen from the menu.

Does it send my location anywhere?

No. Your GPS position and the camera picture are used only on your phone. The only thing that goes online is a place name you type into the "looking from" search, which is looked up by Photon (OpenStreetMap data) or Open-Meteo's place finder.
